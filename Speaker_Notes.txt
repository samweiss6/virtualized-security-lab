SLIDE 1 — TITLE
- Hi, I'm Sam. This is my directed study project: a virtual security lab.
- The one-line version: I built a safe place to attack a computer, and I
  proved that a security monitor can see the whole attack.
- Everything you'll see ran on a single laptop — no cloud, no lab hardware.
[Optional: cut to the 3D replay for 20–30s as a teaser, then come back.]


SLIDE 2 — THE BIG IDEA, IN PLAIN TERMS
- Think of it as three characters: an attacker, a victim, and a watcher.
- The attacker is Kali Linux — the standard hacker toolkit (the penguin).
- The victim is a machine left weak on purpose, so it's safe to break.
- The watcher is a SIEM — it records every packet and shows what happened.
- The whole thing is sealed off: nothing can reach the real internet.
- One constraint shaped everything: it all runs on an 8 GB Apple-M1 laptop —
  which is why I use a native Elastic SIEM, not Splunk (no Apple-chip build;
  emulating the Intel version failed). Realistic: real security work is
  resource-limited.


SLIDE 3 — HOW THE LAB IS WIRED, AND SEALED
- Two virtual machines on a "host-only" network — a private 192.168.128.x
  network that exists only inside the laptop.
- Kali is .2, the target is .4, and the SIEM listens on .1 (the Mac itself).
- Packetbeat mirrors all the traffic into Elastic, so the SIEM sees everything.
- The most important design decision: that dashed box has NO door to the
  internet. "No route" = the VMs can only talk to each other and the Mac;
  traffic aimed at the internet has nowhere to go. Verified, not assumed
  (curl to the internet fails from Kali).
- Why it matters: because it's sealed, I can run real exploits with zero risk —
  the victim can't phone home, spread, or touch the campus network. And I can
  PROVE it later from the SIEM data.


SLIDE 4 — RECON → BREAK IN
- Step 1 is recon: map the target. nmap "knocks on every door" — all 65,535
  ports — and found 23 open, outdated services (old FTP, Samba, an IRC server,
  even a literal root backdoor on port 1524). My scan touched 65,596 distinct
  ports — basically everything. Recon is LOUD; the SIEM is about to light up.
- Step 2 is exploitation: four different ways in, each mapped to MITRE ATT&CK:
  1) FTP: hydra guessed the password (weak credentials).
  2) UnrealIRCd: a known backdoor, CVE-2010-2075 — remote shell.
  3) Samba: command injection, CVE-2007-2447 — remote shell, running as root.
  4) Port 1524: an exposed bind shell — instant root, no password at all.
- Four doors: a weak password, two unpatched services, and a backdoor.
- Tools in one line: nmap (recon), hydra (brute force), Metasploit (exploits),
  netcat (direct shell) — all standard, industry pentest tools.
[If playing the 3D replay: this is the "Recon → Exploit" phase.]


SLIDE 5 — PROOF: WE BECAME ROOT
- This is real output from the attack, not a diagram.
- Top: "uid=0(root)" — that's complete control of the target machine.
  - WHY it means complete control (if asked): uid=0 is "root," the superuser.
    The kernel skips permission checks for root, so it can read/change/delete any
    file, control every service, add or delete users, and even wipe the logs that
    would show the attack. Nothing higher to escalate to — the box is fully owned.
- Bottom: hydra cracking the FTP login, msfadmin / msfadmin, in seconds.
- This is the evidence an industry pentest report would include.


SLIDE 6 — THE SIEM SEES IT ALL
- Now the defender's side. Packetbeat recorded every packet into the SIEM.
- Left chart: the port scan shows up as one giant spike — about 65,000 flows.
- Right chart: roughly 260,000 events captured during the attack window.
- The attack is impossible to miss once you're watching the network.
[If playing the 3D replay: this is the "Detect" phase — streams into the eye.]


SLIDE 7 — EVERY ATTACK → HOW THE SIEM CAUGHT IT
- This table is the heart of the project: each attack lined up with the exact
  signal that caught it.
- Port scan → 65,596 ports. FTP brute force → 72 flows to port 21. The three
  exploits → flows to 6667, 445, and 1524, plus the reverse shells.
- Isolation, proven by the data: the host-to-host table shows only three
  addresses — .1, .2, .4. No public internet address anywhere. The lab is
  genuinely sealed — a measurable fact, not a claim.
- The lesson: preventing attacks is only half the job. DETECTING them is what
  makes this a security lab, not just a hacking exercise.


SLIDE 8 — WHAT IT PROVES & WHAT'S NEXT
- Bottom line: a full security loop — build, attack, detect — on one 8 GB laptop.
- Five real exploits, two of them real-world CVEs, every one captured by the SIEM.
- The hardest part was the Apple-chip workarounds; documenting those failures
  was genuinely the biggest learning.
- Next: custom alert rules, richer dashboards, more attack scenarios, final report.
- Everything — code, evidence, this deck — is on my GitHub. Thank you — questions?


===========================================================================
 Q&A PREP (likely questions)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
- "Why not Splunk?" No Apple-Silicon build; Intel emulation failed. The proposal
  allowed a tool *like* Splunk, so I used Elastic — same SIEM concept.
- "Is it really isolated?" Yes — host-only network, no NAT/bridge; the SIEM data
  shows only lab IPs (slide 7).
- "What does 'no route to the internet' mean?" The VMs have no default gateway, so
  traffic aimed at the internet has nowhere to go; they can only reach each other
  and the Mac. Verified: curl to the internet fails from Kali.
- "Could these attacks hit a real company?" The techniques, yes — that's why they
  map to MITRE ATT&CK. The target is intentionally old; a patched host resists them.
- "What would you fix on the target?" Remove the bind shell, patch/retire the old
  services, enforce strong passwords, segment and monitor (see report §6).
- "How long did it take to boot/attack?" Target boots in ~60s; the full attack
  suite ran in about 12 minutes.
